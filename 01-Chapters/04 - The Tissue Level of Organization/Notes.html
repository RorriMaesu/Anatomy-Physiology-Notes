4.1 Types of Tissues

OpenStax section 4.1

  • The four basic tissue types are epithelial, connective, muscle, and nervous. Histology relates microscopic organization to function.
  • Embryonic germ layers are ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm. Ectoderm gives rise to epidermis and nervous tissue; mesoderm produces much muscle and connective tissue; endoderm forms much digestive and respiratory epithelium.
  • Epithelial membranes combine epithelium with underlying connective tissue: cutaneous membrane is skin, mucous membranes line passages opening outside, and serous membranes line closed ventral cavities and cover organs.
  • Synovial membranes are connective tissue membranes lining joint cavities. They are distinct from epithelial membranes.

Memory cue: ECMN = Every Cell Makes Networks: Epithelial, Connective, Muscle, Nervous. “Ecto exterior and electrical; Meso movement; Endo inner lining” is only a broad developmental cue.

Precision check: An organ contains several tissue types; identifying one tissue in a slide does not classify the whole organ as that tissue.

4.2 Epithelial Tissue

OpenStax section 4.2

  • Epithelium has closely packed cells, apical–basal polarity, attachment to a basement membrane, and little extracellular matrix. It is avascular and receives nutrients by diffusion from underlying tissue.
  • Name epithelia by layers and apical cell shape: simple or stratified, and squamous, cuboidal, or columnar. Pseudostratified looks layered but all cells contact the basement membrane; transitional changes shape during stretching.
  • Thin simple squamous supports exchange; cuboidal and columnar often absorb or secrete; stratified squamous protects. Cilia move surface material; goblet cells secrete mucus.
  • Tight junctions limit passage between cells, anchoring junctions resist separation, and gap junctions allow communication. Endocrine glands release hormones into interstitial fluid; exocrine glands use ducts. Compare merocrine, apocrine, and holocrine secretion.

Make these notes and drawings: Build a slide-identification table for every epithelial type in the section using layers, surface shape, visible clues, location, and function. Draw simple, stratified, pseudostratified, and transitional examples.

Memory cue: Simple = Single layer; Stratified = Stacked. “Mero mails, Apo pinches, Holo whole cell” recalls secretion mechanisms.

Precision check: Classify stratified epithelium by the apical cells, not the deepest cells. Avascular does not mean the tissue has no nerves.

4.3 Connective Tissue Supports and Protects

OpenStax section 4.3

  • Connective tissue contains cells within extracellular matrix made of ground substance and fibers. Collagen resists tension, elastic fibers stretch and recoil, and reticular fibers form supportive networks.
  • Loose connective tissue includes areolar, adipose, and reticular. Dense regular tissue has aligned fibers, as in many tendons; dense irregular tissue resists stress from several directions, as in the reticular dermis.
  • Cartilage contains chondrocytes in lacunae and lacks blood vessels. Hyaline supports smooth, resilient surfaces; elastic cartilage adds flexibility; fibrocartilage withstands compression and tension.
  • Bone has a mineralized matrix; blood has a fluid matrix, plasma. Learn fibroblasts, adipocytes, macrophages, mast cells, and other immune cells as examples of connective tissue cells.

Make these notes and drawings: Build a matrix for areolar, adipose, reticular, dense regular, dense irregular, elastic connective tissue, three cartilages, bone, and blood. Record cells, fiber arrangement, location, and function.

Memory cue: Collagen Cables resist pulls; Elastic Expands and recoils; Reticular Retains a network. Cartilage: Hyaline = support, Elastic = bend, Fibro = force.

Precision check: Blood is connective tissue despite its fluid appearance. Cartilage nutrition depends on diffusion, helping explain limited repair.

4.4 Muscle Tissue and Motion

OpenStax section 4.4

  • Skeletal muscle has long cylindrical striated fibers, many peripheral nuclei, and usually voluntary control. It moves the skeleton, stabilizes joints, and generates heat.
  • Cardiac muscle is striated, branched, and involuntary, with usually one central nucleus and intercalated discs. It propels blood.
  • Smooth muscle has spindle-shaped cells, one central nucleus, and no visible striations. It changes lumen diameter and moves material through hollow organs.
  • All three types produce force through interactions of contractile proteins, but their organization and regulation differ.

Make these notes and drawings: Create a three-row comparison table with shape, nuclei, striations, junctions, control, location, and job. Sketch each type as it would appear in a longitudinal section.

Memory cue: Skeletal = Stripes and Several nuclei; Cardiac = Connected branches; Smooth = Spindle and Single nucleus.

Precision check: Striated does not mean voluntary: cardiac muscle is striated and involuntary. “Smooth” describes microscopic appearance.

4.5 Nervous Tissue Mediates Perception and Response

OpenStax section 4.5

  • Nervous tissue contains neurons and glial cells. Neurons receive, integrate, and transmit information through electrical and chemical signaling.
  • A typical neuron has a soma, dendrites, and an axon. Dendrites commonly receive input; the axon conducts signals toward target cells.
  • Glia maintain the neuronal environment, support metabolism, defend tissue, or form myelin depending on their type. They are active contributors to nervous-system function.

Make these notes and drawings: Draw a neuron and label soma, nucleus, dendrites, axon, and terminals. Place signal-direction arrows. Compare neuron and glial roles and connect a sensory input to a muscle response.

Memory cue: Dendrites Detect; Axons Announce. This captures the typical information route but does not describe every specialized neuron.

Precision check: A nerve is a bundle of axons in the peripheral nervous system; it is not one whole neuron.

4.6 Tissue Injury and Aging

OpenStax section 4.6

  • Tissue injury triggers inflammation, which brings defensive cells and substances to the affected area. Increased blood flow and permeability contribute to redness, warmth, swelling, and pain.
  • Repair may involve regeneration of the original tissue or fibrosis that replaces damaged tissue with scar. Outcome depends on tissue type, injury size, blood supply, and the supporting framework.
  • Follow the broad sequence of clot formation when bleeding occurs, inflammation, new tissue formation, and remodeling. These phases overlap.
  • Aging changes tissue structure and repair capacity. Abnormal proliferation can produce tumors; invasion and metastasis distinguish important malignant behaviors.

Make these notes and drawings: Draw a repair timeline with overlapping phases. Compare regeneration and fibrosis using epidermis and severely injured myocardium. Add normal repair versus uncontrolled growth.

Memory cue: Repair often follows “Close, Clean, Construct, Consolidate”: clot, inflammatory cleanup, new tissue, remodeling. It is a memory model, not a rigid timing rule.

Precision check: Inflammation is not synonymous with infection. Sterile injury can cause inflammation.
