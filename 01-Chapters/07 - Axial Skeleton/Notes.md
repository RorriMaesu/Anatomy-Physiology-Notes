## 7.1 Divisions of the Skeletal System

[OpenStax section 7.1](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/7-1-divisions-of-the-skeletal-system)

- The typical adult skeleton has 206 bones, with normal anatomical variation. The axial skeleton has 80 bones; the appendicular skeleton has 126.
- Axial structures include skull, auditory ossicles, hyoid, vertebral column, ribs, and sternum. Appendicular structures include pectoral and pelvic girdles plus the limbs.
- The axial skeleton supports and protects the central body. The appendicular skeleton supports limb movement and transfers forces between limbs and the trunk.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Color an unlabeled skeleton by division. Reconstruct the 80/126 totals using the chapter’s groups. Create a flashcard for each structure whose division you initially misidentify.

**Memory cue:** Axial = Axis; Appendicular = Appendages and attachments. Remember 80 + 126 = 206 as the standard adult count.

**Precision check:** A girdle belongs to the appendicular skeleton even though it lies close to the trunk. Bone counts vary with age and individual anatomy.

## 7.2 The Skull

[OpenStax section 7.2](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/7-2-the-skull)

- The skull has eight cranial bones and fourteen facial bones. Cranial types are frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital, sphenoid, and ethmoid; parietal and temporal are paired.
- Facial bones include paired maxillae, palatines, zygomatics, nasals, lacrimals, and inferior nasal conchae, plus unpaired vomer and mandible. Locate coronal, sagittal, lambdoid, and squamous sutures.
- Study anterior, lateral, inferior, and internal views. Locate the three cranial fossae, sella turcica, cribriform plate, foramen magnum, major canals and foramina, and mastoid and styloid processes.
- The nasal septum includes ethmoid perpendicular plate, vomer, and septal cartilage. Paranasal sinuses occur in frontal, ethmoid, sphenoid, and maxillary bones. The hyoid is supported by soft tissues rather than articulating directly with another bone.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Make four skull-view labeling sheets. For each opening record bone, location, and transmitted structure when assigned. Build separate orbit, hard-palate, and nasal-septum component lists.

**Memory cue:** “Old People From Texas Eat Spiders” = Occipital, Parietal, Frontal, Temporal, Ethmoid, Sphenoid. These are SIX TYPES; paired P and T give EIGHT bones.

**Precision check:** Superior and middle nasal conchae are parts of the ethmoid; the inferior nasal conchae are separate bones.

## 7.3 The Vertebral Column

[OpenStax section 7.3](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/7-3-the-vertebral-column)

- The column includes seven cervical, twelve thoracic, and five lumbar vertebrae plus sacrum and coccyx. The sacrum usually represents five fused vertebrae; the coccyx usually represents four, with variation.
- A typical vertebra has a body, vertebral arch, vertebral foramen, spinous and transverse processes, and articular processes. The arch contains pedicles and laminae.
- Cervical vertebrae have transverse foramina; thoracic vertebrae bear rib facets; lumbar vertebrae have large weight-bearing bodies. Atlas C1 lacks a typical body; axis C2 has the dens.
- Intervertebral discs have an outer annulus fibrosus and inner nucleus pulposus. Thoracic and sacral curves are primary; cervical and lumbar curves develop after birth. Learn major longitudinal, nuchal, supraspinous, and yellow ligaments.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Draw one typical vertebra and compare cervical, thoracic, lumbar, atlas, and axis. Create a side-view curve diagram, a disc section, and a ligament map.

**Memory cue:** Breakfast at 7, lunch at 12, dinner at 5 = C7, T12, L5. Atlas carries the head; Axis supplies the rotation pivot.

**Precision check:** The vertebral foramen carries the spinal cord region; intervertebral foramina provide routes for spinal nerves. Do not interchange them.

## 7.4 The Thoracic Cage

[OpenStax section 7.4](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/7-4-the-thoracic-cage)

- The thoracic cage consists of sternum, ribs with costal cartilage, and thoracic vertebrae. It protects thoracic organs and participates in breathing movements.
- Sternum components are manubrium, body, and xiphoid process. The sternal angle marks the junction between manubrium and body and helps locate the second rib.
- True ribs 1–7 have direct cartilage connections to the sternum. Ribs 8–12 are false ribs; 8–10 attach indirectly through cartilage, while floating ribs 11–12 lack anterior sternal attachment.
- A typical rib has head, neck, tubercle, angle, shaft, and costal groove. Connect its posterior articular surfaces with the appropriate vertebral surfaces.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Draw the cage and number every rib pair. Use three line styles for direct, indirect, and no anterior attachment. Label a single typical rib and a sternum.

**Memory cue:** 7 true, 3 indirect, 2 floating = 12 pairs. “Manubrium Begins, Xiphoid Ends” helps orient the sternum.

**Precision check:** Floating ribs are a subset of false ribs, not an additional group beyond the twelve pairs.

## 7.5 Embryonic Development of the Axial Skeleton

[OpenStax section 7.5](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/7-5-embryonic-development-of-the-axial-skeleton)

- Much of the cranial vault develops through intramembranous ossification, while much of the skull base develops through endochondral ossification.
- Fontanelles and sutures accommodate deformation during birth and subsequent brain and skull growth. Fontanelles are connective tissue regions, not holes without covering.
- Vertebrae and ribs develop largely from somite-derived sclerotome. Cartilage models are replaced by bone. Growth and fusion continue after birth.
- Premature closure of a cranial suture can redirect skull growth. Developmental explanations connect adult landmarks to their tissue origins.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Draw a fetal skull showing major fontanelles, and compare vault versus base ossification. Add a simple somite-to-sclerotome-to-vertebra/rib sequence. Record closure timings only to the precision assigned.

**Memory cue:** Vault forms in a membrane; Base begins with cartilage. “Fontanelles Flex for birth and growth.”

**Precision check:** Do not memorize a single closure age as an exact deadline for every person. Different sutures and fontanelles have different developmental schedules.
