1.1 Overview of Anatomy and Physiology

OpenStax section 1.1

  • Anatomy describes structure; physiology explains function. Connect each structure to what its shape, position, or material allows it to do. Thin alveolar walls, for example, shorten the distance for gas diffusion.
  • Gross anatomy studies visible structures; microscopic anatomy includes cells and tissues. Regional anatomy groups structures by body area; systemic anatomy follows one organ system across areas.
  • Record a structural observation separately from a functional explanation. Observation, dissection, microscopy, and imaging answer different kinds of anatomical questions.

Make these notes and drawings: Create a comparison table for anatomy, physiology, gross anatomy, and microscopic anatomy. Add one original example to each. Sketch a thin exchange surface and a thick protective surface.

Memory cue: Anatomy = arrangement; physiology = performance. The cue distinguishes the questions asked, but structure and function must be studied together.

Precision check: A label is not an explanation. “The heart has valves” becomes useful when you explain how valves limit backward flow.

1.2 Structural Organization of the Human Body

OpenStax section 1.2

  • Organization proceeds from chemical level to cells, tissues, organs, organ systems, and the organism. A cell is the smallest independently functioning living unit in this hierarchy.
  • A tissue contains related cells and their extracellular material. An organ combines at least two tissue types. An organ system coordinates organs to perform major functions.
  • Learn the 11 systems with a representative organ and function: integumentary, skeletal, muscular, nervous, endocrine, cardiovascular, lymphatic and immune, respiratory, digestive, urinary, reproductive. An organ can contribute to more than one system.

Make these notes and drawings: Make a six-level ladder using one example that connects to the next, such as protein → muscle cell → cardiac muscle tissue → heart → cardiovascular system → person. Build an 11-row system table.

Memory cue: Chemicals Create Tissues; Organs Organize Organisms = Chemical, Cellular, Tissue, Organ, Organ system, Organism. Use the exact terms when recalling the ladder.

Precision check: A tissue is not simply “many identical cells”; extracellular material can be a major component, especially in connective tissue.

1.3 Functions of Human Life

OpenStax section 1.3

  • Organization maintains boundaries and internal order. Metabolism is the sum of chemical reactions; anabolism builds larger molecules and catabolism breaks molecules down.
  • Responsiveness detects and reacts to change. Movement includes the whole body, organs, cells, and movement within cells.
  • Development includes growth and differentiation. Growth increases size; differentiation produces specialized structure and function. Reproduction can mean producing new cells or a new organism.

Make these notes and drawings: Create a function–definition–example table. Use protein synthesis for anabolism, glucose breakdown for catabolism, and a stem cell becoming a muscle cell for differentiation. Link each example to an organ system.

Memory cue: A builds Above; C Cuts: Anabolism builds and Catabolism breaks down. “Organized Metabolism Responds, Moves, Develops, Reproduces” recalls the broader life-function headings.

Precision check: Growth and differentiation are different. A cell can specialize without simply becoming larger.

1.4 Requirements for Human Life

OpenStax section 1.4

  • Human cells need oxygen for efficient aerobic ATP production, nutrients for energy and materials, and water as a solvent and reaction medium.
  • Body temperature must stay within a tolerable range because temperature affects reaction rates and protein structure. Excessive heat can disrupt proteins; cold slows reactions.
  • Atmospheric pressure supports normal breathing and oxygen availability. A pressure change can affect gas behavior; requirements for life interact rather than operating independently.

Make these notes and drawings: Write four headings: oxygen, nutrients, temperature, pressure. Under each, explain the cellular need and predict a consequence of a major change. Include water under nutrients.

Memory cue: Oxygen Nourishes Temperature-sensitive People = Oxygen, Nutrients, Temperature, Pressure. The phrase recalls categories; nutrients do not literally control every temperature change.

Precision check: Oxygen supplies neither calories nor the carbon skeletons of food. Its role in aerobic respiration differs from the roles of nutrients.

1.5 Homeostasis

OpenStax section 1.5

  • Homeostasis maintains internal conditions within workable ranges. A regulated variable fluctuates around a set point or range; it does not remain perfectly constant.
  • A receptor detects change, a control center integrates information, and an effector produces a response. Negative feedback reduces the original deviation, such as sweating when temperature rises.
  • Positive feedback amplifies a process until an endpoint interrupts the loop. Childbirth and blood clotting illustrate amplification; they are not everyday mechanisms for holding a variable near a set point.

Make these notes and drawings: Draw and label both feedback loops. For temperature regulation identify stimulus, receptor, control center, effector, response, and how the response changes the original stimulus. Add a cold-response branch.

Memory cue: RCE = Receptor Calls Effector, with the Control center interpreting the call. Negative Negates the deviation; Positive Promotes the process.

Precision check: Negative and positive describe feedback direction, not whether the outcome is good or bad. The regulated variable must be named.

1.6 Anatomical Terminology

OpenStax section 1.6

  • Use anatomical position: upright, facing forward, arms at the sides, palms forward. Right and left belong to the subject. Supine means face up; prone means face down.
  • Learn paired directions: superior/inferior, anterior/posterior, medial/lateral, proximal/distal, superficial/deep. Sagittal planes divide right and left; frontal divides anterior/posterior; transverse divides superior/inferior.
  • Dorsal cavities include cranial and vertebral. Ventral cavities include thoracic and abdominopelvic. The diaphragm separates thoracic and abdominal cavities. Visceral serosa covers an organ; parietal serosa lines the cavity wall.
  • Label four abdominal quadrants and nine regions. Learn regional terms from the textbook anterior and posterior body figures, including brachial, antebrachial, femoral, crural, popliteal, and sural.

Make these notes and drawings: Draw front and back body outlines, three cutting planes, a cavity map, and a nine-region abdominal grid. Write six complete comparison sentences such as “The elbow is proximal to the wrist.”

Memory cue: Visceral Visits the organ; Parietal is the Partition wall. Proximal = proximity to attachment; Distal = distant from attachment.

Precision check: Proximal/distal follow attachment along a limb; they are not interchangeable with superior/inferior. Pleural and pericardial cavities are thin potential spaces.

1.7 Medical Imaging

OpenStax section 1.7

  • Radiography uses X-rays to show differences in absorption. Dense tissues such as bone appear relatively radiopaque. CT combines X-ray projections into cross-sectional images.
  • MRI uses magnetic fields and radiofrequency signals to distinguish tissues. Ultrasound uses reflected sound waves and supports real-time imaging.
  • PET detects signals associated with a radioactive tracer and reveals aspects of tissue activity. Compare structural detail, functional information, radiation type, and practical limitations for each method.

Make these notes and drawings: Make a five-row table for X-ray, CT, MRI, ultrasound, and PET. Record the physical principle and a textbook use. Identify which methods use ionizing radiation.

Memory cue: CT = Computed slices; MRI = Magnet; Ultrasound = echoes; PET = tracer activity. These are recall hooks, not full technical definitions.

Precision check: MRI does not use X-rays. Ultrasound also avoids ionizing radiation, but imaging suitability depends on the body part and question.
