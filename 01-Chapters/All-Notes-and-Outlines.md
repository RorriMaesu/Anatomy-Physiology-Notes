## 1.1 Overview of Anatomy and Physiology

[OpenStax section 1.1](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/1-1-overview-of-anatomy-and-physiology)

- Anatomy describes structure; physiology explains function. Connect each structure to what its shape, position, or material allows it to do. Thin alveolar walls, for example, shorten the distance for gas diffusion.
- Gross anatomy studies visible structures; microscopic anatomy includes cells and tissues. Regional anatomy groups structures by body area; systemic anatomy follows one organ system across areas.
- Record a structural observation separately from a functional explanation. Observation, dissection, microscopy, and imaging answer different kinds of anatomical questions.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Create a comparison table for anatomy, physiology, gross anatomy, and microscopic anatomy. Add one original example to each. Sketch a thin exchange surface and a thick protective surface.

**Memory cue:** Anatomy = arrangement; physiology = performance. The cue distinguishes the questions asked, but structure and function must be studied together.

**Precision check:** A label is not an explanation. “The heart has valves” becomes useful when you explain how valves limit backward flow.


## 1.2 Structural Organization of the Human Body

[OpenStax section 1.2](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/1-2-structural-organization-of-the-human-body)

- Organization proceeds from chemical level to cells, tissues, organs, organ systems, and the organism. A cell is the smallest independently functioning living unit in this hierarchy.
- A tissue contains related cells and their extracellular material. An organ combines at least two tissue types. An organ system coordinates organs to perform major functions.
- Learn the 11 systems with a representative organ and function: integumentary, skeletal, muscular, nervous, endocrine, cardiovascular, lymphatic and immune, respiratory, digestive, urinary, reproductive. An organ can contribute to more than one system.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Make a six-level ladder using one example that connects to the next, such as protein → muscle cell → cardiac muscle tissue → heart → cardiovascular system → person. Build an 11-row system table.

**Memory cue:** Chemicals Create Tissues; Organs Organize Organisms = Chemical, Cellular, Tissue, Organ, Organ system, Organism. Use the exact terms when recalling the ladder.

**Precision check:** A tissue is not simply “many identical cells”; extracellular material can be a major component, especially in connective tissue.


## 1.3 Functions of Human Life

[OpenStax section 1.3](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/1-3-functions-of-human-life)

- Organization maintains boundaries and internal order. Metabolism is the sum of chemical reactions; anabolism builds larger molecules and catabolism breaks molecules down.
- Responsiveness detects and reacts to change. Movement includes the whole body, organs, cells, and movement within cells.
- Development includes growth and differentiation. Growth increases size; differentiation produces specialized structure and function. Reproduction can mean producing new cells or a new organism.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Create a function–definition–example table. Use protein synthesis for anabolism, glucose breakdown for catabolism, and a stem cell becoming a muscle cell for differentiation. Link each example to an organ system.

**Memory cue:** A builds Above; C Cuts: Anabolism builds and Catabolism breaks down. “Organized Metabolism Responds, Moves, Develops, Reproduces” recalls the broader life-function headings.

**Precision check:** Growth and differentiation are different. A cell can specialize without simply becoming larger.


## 1.4 Requirements for Human Life

[OpenStax section 1.4](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/1-4-requirements-for-human-life)

- Human cells need oxygen for efficient aerobic ATP production, nutrients for energy and materials, and water as a solvent and reaction medium.
- Body temperature must stay within a tolerable range because temperature affects reaction rates and protein structure. Excessive heat can disrupt proteins; cold slows reactions.
- Atmospheric pressure supports normal breathing and oxygen availability. A pressure change can affect gas behavior; requirements for life interact rather than operating independently.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Write four headings: oxygen, nutrients, temperature, pressure. Under each, explain the cellular need and predict a consequence of a major change. Include water under nutrients.

**Memory cue:** Oxygen Nourishes Temperature-sensitive People = Oxygen, Nutrients, Temperature, Pressure. The phrase recalls categories; nutrients do not literally control every temperature change.

**Precision check:** Oxygen supplies neither calories nor the carbon skeletons of food. Its role in aerobic respiration differs from the roles of nutrients.


## 1.5 Homeostasis

[OpenStax section 1.5](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/1-5-homeostasis)

- Homeostasis maintains internal conditions within workable ranges. A regulated variable fluctuates around a set point or range; it does not remain perfectly constant.
- A receptor detects change, a control center integrates information, and an effector produces a response. Negative feedback reduces the original deviation, such as sweating when temperature rises.
- Positive feedback amplifies a process until an endpoint interrupts the loop. Childbirth and blood clotting illustrate amplification; they are not everyday mechanisms for holding a variable near a set point.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Draw and label both feedback loops. For temperature regulation identify stimulus, receptor, control center, effector, response, and how the response changes the original stimulus. Add a cold-response branch.

**Memory cue:** RCE = Receptor Calls Effector, with the Control center interpreting the call. Negative Negates the deviation; Positive Promotes the process.

**Precision check:** Negative and positive describe feedback direction, not whether the outcome is good or bad. The regulated variable must be named.


## 1.6 Anatomical Terminology

[OpenStax section 1.6](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/1-6-anatomical-terminology)

- Use anatomical position: upright, facing forward, arms at the sides, palms forward. Right and left belong to the subject. Supine means face up; prone means face down.
- Learn paired directions: superior/inferior, anterior/posterior, medial/lateral, proximal/distal, superficial/deep. Sagittal planes divide right and left; frontal divides anterior/posterior; transverse divides superior/inferior.
- Dorsal cavities include cranial and vertebral. Ventral cavities include thoracic and abdominopelvic. The diaphragm separates thoracic and abdominal cavities. Visceral serosa covers an organ; parietal serosa lines the cavity wall.
- Label four abdominal quadrants and nine regions. Learn regional terms from the textbook anterior and posterior body figures, including brachial, antebrachial, femoral, crural, popliteal, and sural.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Draw front and back body outlines, three cutting planes, a cavity map, and a nine-region abdominal grid. Write six complete comparison sentences such as “The elbow is proximal to the wrist.”

**Memory cue:** Visceral Visits the organ; Parietal is the Partition wall. Proximal = proximity to attachment; Distal = distant from attachment.

**Precision check:** Proximal/distal follow attachment along a limb; they are not interchangeable with superior/inferior. Pleural and pericardial cavities are thin potential spaces.


## 1.7 Medical Imaging

[OpenStax section 1.7](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/1-7-medical-imaging)

- Radiography uses X-rays to show differences in absorption. Dense tissues such as bone appear relatively radiopaque. CT combines X-ray projections into cross-sectional images.
- MRI uses magnetic fields and radiofrequency signals to distinguish tissues. Ultrasound uses reflected sound waves and supports real-time imaging.
- PET detects signals associated with a radioactive tracer and reveals aspects of tissue activity. Compare structural detail, functional information, radiation type, and practical limitations for each method.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Make a five-row table for X-ray, CT, MRI, ultrasound, and PET. Record the physical principle and a textbook use. Identify which methods use ionizing radiation.

**Memory cue:** CT = Computed slices; MRI = Magnet; Ultrasound = echoes; PET = tracer activity. These are recall hooks, not full technical definitions.

**Precision check:** MRI does not use X-rays. Ultrasound also avoids ionizing radiation, but imaging suitability depends on the body part and question.


## Chapter 2 The Chemical Level of Organization

Detailed notes. Match these sections to your actual reading assignments.

## 2.1 Elements and Atoms: The Building Blocks of Matter

[OpenStax section 2.1](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/2-1-elements-and-atoms-the-building-blocks-of-matter)

- Matter has mass and occupies space. An element contains one type of atom; a compound contains chemically combined elements. Oxygen, carbon, hydrogen, and nitrogen account for most body mass.
- Protons are positive and neutrons neutral in the nucleus; electrons are negative outside it. Atomic number equals proton number. Mass number equals protons plus neutrons.
- Isotopes have the same proton number but different neutron numbers. Ions differ in electron number. Valence electrons strongly influence bonding and chemical reactivity.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Draw a simple atom and label charges and locations. Calculate proton, neutron, and electron counts for carbon-12 and a sodium ion. Compare element, compound, isotope, and ion.

**Memory cue:** P is Positive; N is Neutral. “Mass = Massive nuclear particles” recalls protons plus neutrons. CHON names the major body elements.

**Precision check:** Do not confuse mass number of one isotope with an element’s average atomic mass. Changing protons changes the element.


## 2.2 Chemical Bonds

[OpenStax section 2.2](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/2-2-chemical-bonds)

- Ionic attraction occurs between oppositely charged ions. Electron transfer can produce a cation, which is positive, and an anion, which is negative.
- Covalent bonds share electron pairs. Equal sharing is nonpolar; unequal sharing is polar. Water’s polar covalent bonds give it partial positive and negative regions.
- Hydrogen bonds are attractions involving a partially positive hydrogen and an electronegative atom. Many weak interactions together help stabilize water networks, protein shapes, and DNA base pairing.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Sketch electron transfer for sodium/chloride, shared electrons for a covalent bond, and two water molecules connected by a hydrogen bond. Mark full ionic charges separately from partial charges.

**Memory cue:** Covalent = Co-own electrons. ANion is Negative. Hydrogen bonds Hold neighbors together.

**Precision check:** The O–H bonds within one water molecule are covalent. A hydrogen bond between water molecules is a different interaction.


## 2.3 Chemical Reactions

[OpenStax section 2.3](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/2-3-chemical-reactions)

- Reactants are starting substances; products are formed. Synthesis builds, decomposition breaks down, and exchange swaps components. Reversible reactions can proceed in either direction.
- Potential energy is stored by position or chemical arrangement; kinetic energy is energy of motion. Exergonic reactions release net usable energy; endergonic reactions require net energy input.
- Reaction rate depends on reactant properties, concentration, temperature, and catalysts. Enzymes lower activation energy and are not consumed by the reaction.
- Breaking a chemical bond requires energy; forming bonds releases energy. The overall energy change depends on both processes. ATP hydrolysis has a favorable net energy change under cellular conditions.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Write one symbolic example each of synthesis, decomposition, exchange, and reversibility. Draw an energy diagram with and without an enzyme and label activation energy.

**Memory cue:** Synthesis Stacks; Decomposition Divides; Exchange Exchanges. Enzymes Ease the energy barrier.

**Precision check:** An enzyme changes the rate, not the reaction’s overall energy difference or its equilibrium position.


## 2.4 Inorganic Compounds Essential to Human Functioning

[OpenStax section 2.4](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/2-4-inorganic-compounds-essential-to-human-functioning)

- Water dissolves many polar and ionic substances, absorbs heat, lubricates surfaces, and participates in reactions. Hydrolysis uses water to split molecules; dehydration synthesis releases water while joining them.
- A solution contains solute dispersed in solvent. Suspensions have larger particles that can settle; colloids have intermediate dispersed particles. Record concentration units carefully.
- Salts dissociate into ions in water. Electrolytes conduct current in solution. Acids donate hydrogen ions; bases accept hydrogen ions or reduce their availability.
- pH is logarithmic: a one-unit decrease means ten times more H+. Buffers resist pH change by reversibly accepting or releasing H+. Normal arterial blood pH is approximately 7.35–7.45.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Build a water-functions table and a pH number line. Work pH comparisons of 7 versus 6 and 7 versus 5. Draw a buffer accepting excess H+.

**Memory cue:** Hydrolysis = Hydro helps split. “Lower pH, more H” recalls direction. One pH step is tenfold.

**Precision check:** A buffer limits change; it does not make every solution neutral or prevent all possible pH changes.


## 2.5 Organic Compounds Essential to Human Functioning

[OpenStax section 2.5](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/2-5-organic-compounds-essential-to-human-functioning)

- Carbohydrates include monosaccharides, disaccharides, and polysaccharides; glucose fuels cells and glycogen stores glucose in animals. Dehydration and hydrolysis connect building and breakdown.
- Lipids include triglycerides for energy storage, phospholipids for membranes, steroids such as cholesterol, and signaling lipids. Saturated fatty acids lack carbon–carbon double bonds.
- Proteins are amino-acid chains linked by peptide bonds. Primary sequence supports secondary, tertiary, and sometimes quaternary structure. Shape determines binding and function; denaturation disrupts higher-level structure.
- Nucleotides contain sugar, phosphate, and a nitrogenous base. DNA stores hereditary information; RNA helps express it. ATP couples energy-releasing and energy-requiring processes.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Create a four-family table with building blocks, bonds or structure, major functions, and examples. Sketch a phospholipid and nucleotide. Add DNA/RNA sugar and base comparisons.

**Memory cue:** Carbs fuel, Lipids store and line, Proteins perform, Nucleic acids encode. “Sequence → Shape → Service” connects protein levels to function.

**Precision check:** Lipids are not all polymers of a single repeating monomer. A steroid has fused rings, whereas a triglyceride has glycerol plus three fatty acids.


## Chapter 3 The Cellular Level of Organization

Detailed notes. Match these sections to your actual reading assignments.

## 3.1 The Cell Membrane

[OpenStax section 3.1](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/3-1-the-cell-membrane)

- The plasma membrane is a fluid phospholipid bilayer with proteins, cholesterol, and surface carbohydrates. Its selective permeability separates intracellular and extracellular environments.
- Simple diffusion moves substances down a gradient through the bilayer; facilitated diffusion uses channels or carriers without direct energy input. Osmosis is net water movement across a selectively permeable membrane.
- Primary active transport directly uses energy such as ATP; secondary active transport uses an existing ion gradient. The Na+/K+ pump exports three Na+ and imports two K+ per ATP. Endocytosis brings material in; exocytosis releases it.
- Tonicity predicts cell-volume change from nonpenetrating solutes. A hypotonic external solution makes a typical animal cell swell; hypertonic makes it shrink; isotonic produces no sustained net volume change.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Draw the bilayer and a transport comparison chart with cargo, direction, protein requirement, and energy source. Sketch cells in three tonicities and label the solution relative to the cell.

**Memory cue:** Hypo = Hippo cell; Hyper = shriveled cell. “3 sodium Out, 2 potassium In” is the pump ratio.

**Precision check:** At equilibrium molecules still move. Isotonic does not mean water stops moving. Osmolarity and tonicity are not always identical.


## 3.2 The Cytoplasm and Cellular Organelles

[OpenStax section 3.2](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/3-2-the-cytoplasm-and-cellular-organelles)

- Cytoplasm includes cytosol and organelles outside the nucleus. Free ribosomes synthesize many proteins used in the cytosol; ribosomes on rough ER make many secreted, membrane, and endomembrane proteins.
- Rough ER folds and processes proteins; smooth ER participates in lipid synthesis, detoxification, and calcium storage in specialized cells. Golgi modifies, sorts, and packages products.
- Lysosomes digest material; peroxisomes carry out oxidative reactions and break down hydrogen peroxide. Mitochondria support aerobic ATP production.
- Microfilaments, intermediate filaments, and microtubules provide structure and movement. Cilia move material across surfaces; a flagellum propels a sperm cell; microvilli increase surface area.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Make an organelle–structure–function table. Trace a secreted protein from ribosome to rough ER, transport vesicle, Golgi, secretory vesicle, and plasma membrane. Compare cilia with microvilli.

**Memory cue:** Rough ER = Ribosomes; Golgi = packaging and shipping; Lysosomes = digestion; Peroxisomes = peroxide processing. These analogies support, but do not replace, the specific functions.

**Precision check:** Ribosomes are not membrane-bound. Mitochondria are not the only site where cells generate ATP.


## 3.3 The Nucleus and DNA Replication

[OpenStax section 3.3](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/3-3-the-nucleus-and-dna-replication)

- The nuclear envelope has two membranes and pores that regulate exchange. The nucleolus assembles ribosomal components. Chromatin consists of DNA and associated proteins.
- DNA has antiparallel strands with sugar–phosphate backbones and complementary bases: A pairs with T, C with G. A gene is a DNA sequence used to produce a functional RNA or protein product.
- Replication is semiconservative: each daughter DNA molecule retains one old strand and gains one new strand. Helicase separates strands, DNA polymerase extends new DNA, and ligase joins DNA fragments.
- New DNA is synthesized 5′ to 3′. The leading strand is made continuously; the lagging strand forms fragments. DNA replication occurs during S phase before cell division.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Draw a nucleus, a DNA ladder, and a replication fork. Mark old versus new strands, direction, leading/lagging strands, polymerase, and ligase. Practice complementary sequences.

**Memory cue:** AT the Car Garage = A–T and C–G. Helicase unHelixes; Polymerase Produces; Ligase Links.

**Precision check:** DNA copying does not double the chromosome count immediately. It produces sister chromatids attached at a centromere.


## 3.4 Protein Synthesis

[OpenStax section 3.4](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/3-4-protein-synthesis)

- Transcription produces RNA from a DNA template. In eukaryotic cells, pre-mRNA processing includes a cap, poly-A tail, and removal of introns by splicing before export.
- Translation uses ribosomes to read mRNA codons. tRNA anticodons pair with codons and deliver amino acids. Peptide bonds link the amino acids into a polypeptide.
- AUG commonly initiates translation and codes for methionine. Stop codons signal termination without adding an amino acid. The resulting polypeptide must fold and may undergo processing.
- Distinguish DNA template sequence, coding sequence, mRNA, tRNA anticodon, and amino-acid sequence. RNA uses U instead of T.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Draw nucleus-to-cytoplasm information flow. Practice one short example: DNA template 3′-TAC-5′ → mRNA 5′-AUG-3′ → methionine. Label transcription and translation at different locations.

**Memory cue:** Transcription = Transcribe into RNA; Translation = Translate into protein. “Introns Interrupt; Exons Express” recalls splicing, although some retained exons are untranslated.

**Precision check:** Read a genetic code table using the mRNA codon, not the DNA template or tRNA anticodon.


## 3.5 Cell Growth and Division

[OpenStax section 3.5](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/3-5-cell-growth-and-division)

- The cell cycle includes G1 growth, S DNA replication, G2 preparation, and M phase. Some cells enter G0, a nondividing state. Interphase includes G1, S, and G2.
- Mitosis separates duplicated chromosomes: prophase condenses them, metaphase aligns them, anaphase separates sister chromatids, and telophase forms daughter nuclei. Cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm.
- Cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases help regulate progression. Checkpoints assess conditions such as DNA integrity and chromosome attachment. Failed controls can permit uncontrolled proliferation.
- A duplicated chromosome has two sister chromatids. After separation, each chromatid is considered a chromosome. For an ordinary human somatic cell, each daughter nucleus normally receives 46 chromosomes.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Draw a circular cell-cycle map and four mitotic panels. Track one homologous pair using two colors, then show duplication and distribution. Add checkpoints and distinguish mitosis from cytokinesis.

**Memory cue:** PMAT = Please Make Another Two: Prophase, Metaphase, Anaphase, Telophase. Metaphase = Middle; Anaphase = Apart.

**Precision check:** Interphase is active growth and preparation, not a universal resting period. Mitosis and the whole cell cycle are not synonyms.


## 3.6 Cellular Differentiation

[OpenStax section 3.6](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/3-6-cellular-differentiation)

- Differentiation gives cells specialized structures and functions by changing patterns of gene expression. Most nucleated somatic cells retain largely the same genome but use different subsets of genes.
- Stem cells can self-renew and generate more specialized descendants. Potency describes the range of cell types they can produce: totipotent, pluripotent, multipotent, or more restricted.
- Totipotent cells can contribute to the embryo and supporting extraembryonic tissues; pluripotent cells can form the body’s major cell types. Adult tissue stem cells are usually more restricted.
- Cell signals and transcription factors help activate or silence gene programs. Specialized structure, such as a neuron’s long axon, supports a specialized job.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Make a potency hierarchy with definitions and examples from the section. Compare a neuron and muscle fiber under genes present, genes expressed, structure, and function.

**Memory cue:** T → P → M = Total potential, Plenty of body types, Multiple related types. Use the formal definitions rather than treating the cue as a precise potency test.

**Precision check:** Differentiation usually does not mean the cell discards every gene it no longer expresses.

## 4.1 Types of Tissues

[OpenStax section 4.1](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/4-1-types-of-tissues)

- The four basic tissue types are epithelial, connective, muscle, and nervous. Histology relates microscopic organization to function.
- Embryonic germ layers are ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm. Ectoderm gives rise to epidermis and nervous tissue; mesoderm produces much muscle and connective tissue; endoderm forms much digestive and respiratory epithelium.
- Epithelial membranes combine epithelium with underlying connective tissue: cutaneous membrane is skin, mucous membranes line passages opening outside, and serous membranes line closed ventral cavities and cover organs.
- Synovial membranes are connective tissue membranes lining joint cavities. They are distinct from epithelial membranes.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Draw a four-tissue concept map. Build a membrane table with tissue composition, location, secretion, and function. Add a three-germ-layer diagram with representative derivatives.

**Memory cue:** ECMN = Every Cell Makes Networks: Epithelial, Connective, Muscle, Nervous. “Ecto exterior and electrical; Meso movement; Endo inner lining” is only a broad developmental cue.

**Precision check:** An organ contains several tissue types; identifying one tissue in a slide does not classify the whole organ as that tissue.


## 4.2 Epithelial Tissue

[OpenStax section 4.2](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/4-2-epithelial-tissue)

- Epithelium has closely packed cells, apical–basal polarity, attachment to a basement membrane, and little extracellular matrix. It is avascular and receives nutrients by diffusion from underlying tissue.
- Name epithelia by layers and apical cell shape: simple or stratified, and squamous, cuboidal, or columnar. Pseudostratified looks layered but all cells contact the basement membrane; transitional changes shape during stretching.
- Thin simple squamous supports exchange; cuboidal and columnar often absorb or secrete; stratified squamous protects. Cilia move surface material; goblet cells secrete mucus.
- Tight junctions limit passage between cells, anchoring junctions resist separation, and gap junctions allow communication. Endocrine glands release hormones into interstitial fluid; exocrine glands use ducts. Compare merocrine, apocrine, and holocrine secretion.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Build a slide-identification table for every epithelial type in the section using layers, surface shape, visible clues, location, and function. Draw simple, stratified, pseudostratified, and transitional examples.

**Memory cue:** Simple = Single layer; Stratified = Stacked. “Mero mails, Apo pinches, Holo whole cell” recalls secretion mechanisms.

**Precision check:** Classify stratified epithelium by the apical cells, not the deepest cells. Avascular does not mean the tissue has no nerves.


## 4.3 Connective Tissue Supports and Protects

[OpenStax section 4.3](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/4-3-connective-tissue-supports-and-protects)

- Connective tissue contains cells within extracellular matrix made of ground substance and fibers. Collagen resists tension, elastic fibers stretch and recoil, and reticular fibers form supportive networks.
- Loose connective tissue includes areolar, adipose, and reticular. Dense regular tissue has aligned fibers, as in many tendons; dense irregular tissue resists stress from several directions, as in the reticular dermis.
- Cartilage contains chondrocytes in lacunae and lacks blood vessels. Hyaline supports smooth, resilient surfaces; elastic cartilage adds flexibility; fibrocartilage withstands compression and tension.
- Bone has a mineralized matrix; blood has a fluid matrix, plasma. Learn fibroblasts, adipocytes, macrophages, mast cells, and other immune cells as examples of connective tissue cells.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Build a matrix for areolar, adipose, reticular, dense regular, dense irregular, elastic connective tissue, three cartilages, bone, and blood. Record cells, fiber arrangement, location, and function.

**Memory cue:** Collagen Cables resist pulls; Elastic Expands and recoils; Reticular Retains a network. Cartilage: Hyaline = support, Elastic = bend, Fibro = force.

**Precision check:** Blood is connective tissue despite its fluid appearance. Cartilage nutrition depends on diffusion, helping explain limited repair.


## 4.4 Muscle Tissue and Motion

[OpenStax section 4.4](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/4-4-muscle-tissue-and-motion)

- Skeletal muscle has long cylindrical striated fibers, many peripheral nuclei, and usually voluntary control. It moves the skeleton, stabilizes joints, and generates heat.
- Cardiac muscle is striated, branched, and involuntary, with usually one central nucleus and intercalated discs. It propels blood.
- Smooth muscle has spindle-shaped cells, one central nucleus, and no visible striations. It changes lumen diameter and moves material through hollow organs.
- All three types produce force through interactions of contractile proteins, but their organization and regulation differ.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Create a three-row comparison table with shape, nuclei, striations, junctions, control, location, and job. Sketch each type as it would appear in a longitudinal section.

**Memory cue:** Skeletal = Stripes and Several nuclei; Cardiac = Connected branches; Smooth = Spindle and Single nucleus.

**Precision check:** Striated does not mean voluntary: cardiac muscle is striated and involuntary. “Smooth” describes microscopic appearance.


## 4.5 Nervous Tissue Mediates Perception and Response

[OpenStax section 4.5](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/4-5-nervous-tissue-mediates-perception-and-response)

- Nervous tissue contains neurons and glial cells. Neurons receive, integrate, and transmit information through electrical and chemical signaling.
- A typical neuron has a soma, dendrites, and an axon. Dendrites commonly receive input; the axon conducts signals toward target cells.
- Glia maintain the neuronal environment, support metabolism, defend tissue, or form myelin depending on their type. They are active contributors to nervous-system function.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Draw a neuron and label soma, nucleus, dendrites, axon, and terminals. Place signal-direction arrows. Compare neuron and glial roles and connect a sensory input to a muscle response.

**Memory cue:** Dendrites Detect; Axons Announce. This captures the typical information route but does not describe every specialized neuron.

**Precision check:** A nerve is a bundle of axons in the peripheral nervous system; it is not one whole neuron.


## 4.6 Tissue Injury and Aging

[OpenStax section 4.6](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/4-6-tissue-injury-and-aging)

- Tissue injury triggers inflammation, which brings defensive cells and substances to the affected area. Increased blood flow and permeability contribute to redness, warmth, swelling, and pain.
- Repair may involve regeneration of the original tissue or fibrosis that replaces damaged tissue with scar. Outcome depends on tissue type, injury size, blood supply, and the supporting framework.
- Follow the broad sequence of clot formation when bleeding occurs, inflammation, new tissue formation, and remodeling. These phases overlap.
- Aging changes tissue structure and repair capacity. Abnormal proliferation can produce tumors; invasion and metastasis distinguish important malignant behaviors.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Draw a repair timeline with overlapping phases. Compare regeneration and fibrosis using epidermis and severely injured myocardium. Add normal repair versus uncontrolled growth.

**Memory cue:** Repair often follows “Close, Clean, Construct, Consolidate”: clot, inflammatory cleanup, new tissue, remodeling. It is a memory model, not a rigid timing rule.

**Precision check:** Inflammation is not synonymous with infection. Sterile injury can cause inflammation.

## 5.1 Layers of the Skin

[OpenStax section 5.1](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/5-1-layers-of-the-skin)

- Skin consists of epidermis and dermis. The hypodermis lies beneath skin and contains areolar and adipose tissue. The epidermis is avascular keratinized stratified squamous epithelium.
- From deep to superficial, epidermal strata are basale, spinosum, granulosum, lucidum when present, and corneum. Thick skin on palms and soles has lucidum and lacks hair follicles.
- Keratinocytes form the protective barrier; melanocytes produce melanin; epidermal dendritic cells support defense; tactile epithelial cells contribute to touch sensation.
- Papillary dermis contains areolar connective tissue; reticular dermis contains dense irregular connective tissue. Blood supply, nerves, and many accessory structures lie in the dermis. Pigmentation reflects melanin, carotene, and blood-related influences.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Draw a skin section with every stratum, both dermal layers, hypodermis, vessels, and major cell types. Compare thick and thin skin. Connect surface cell loss with basal replacement.

**Memory cue:** “Come Let’s Get Sun Burned” = Corneum, Lucidum, Granulosum, Spinosum, Basale, SUPERFICIAL to DEEP. Omit Lucidum for thin skin.

**Precision check:** The reticular dermis is dense irregular connective tissue. The hypodermis is not an epidermal layer.


## 5.2 Accessory Structures of the Skin

[OpenStax section 5.2](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/5-2-accessory-structures-of-the-skin)

- Hair develops from dividing matrix cells in a follicle; the visible shaft is dead keratinized material. Label root, bulb, papilla, matrix, and arrector pili. Hair cycles through growth, regression, and rest.
- Nails grow from a matrix and contain hard keratin. Distinguish nail plate, bed, root, lunula, and cuticle.
- Eccrine sweat glands open onto the skin surface and support cooling. Apocrine sweat glands usually empty into hair follicles in selected areas and become active around puberty.
- Sebaceous glands release lipid-rich sebum, usually into follicles, by holocrine secretion. Their lubricating secretion differs from watery sweat.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Make a hair-and-nail labeling page plus a gland comparison table with secretion, outlet, location, and function. Draw an eccrine duct to the surface and sebaceous duct to a follicle.

## 5.3 Functions of the Integumentary System

[OpenStax section 5.3](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/5-3-functions-of-the-integumentary-system)

- The skin limits water loss and protects against mechanical injury, chemicals, microbes, and some ultraviolet damage. Barrier function depends on cells, keratin, lipids, and immune defenses.
- Receptors detect touch, pressure, vibration, temperature, and potentially damaging stimuli. Different receptors occupy different depths and have different response properties.
- Sweating cools through evaporation. Dermal vasodilation increases heat transfer toward the surface; vasoconstriction reduces it. Hypodermal fat contributes insulation.
- Ultraviolet exposure initiates production of a vitamin D precursor in skin; liver and kidney processing contributes to formation of active vitamin D. Skin aging affects barrier, elasticity, sensation, and repair.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Draw warm- and cold-response feedback loops. Make a structure–function table linking keratin, melanin, blood vessels, glands, receptors, and adipose tissue to their jobs.

**Memory cue:** PSTV = Protect, Sense, Thermoregulate, Vitamin D. “Dilate to Dissipate” recalls heat loss through increased skin blood flow.

**Precision check:** Sweat must evaporate to provide effective cooling. Sweat production alone is not the same as heat removal.


## 5.4 Diseases, Disorders, and Injuries of the Integumentary System

## 6.1 The Functions of the Skeletal System

[OpenStax section 6.1](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/6-1-the-functions-of-the-skeletal-system)

- Bones support the body and act as levers for skeletal muscles. They protect structures such as brain, spinal cord, thoracic organs, and pelvic organs.
- Bone stores calcium and phosphate and exchanges minerals with body fluids. Red marrow performs hematopoiesis, the production of blood cells.
- Yellow marrow stores triglycerides. The distribution of red and yellow marrow changes with age and anatomical location.
- The skeletal system includes bones plus associated cartilages and ligaments. Its functions depend on interactions with muscular, circulatory, endocrine, and other systems.

**Memory cue:** “Support Protects Movers; Minerals, Blood, Fat” recalls support, protection, movement, mineral storage, hematopoiesis, and fat storage.

**Precision check:** Bone is living, vascular tissue. A skeleton is not merely a static framework of mineral.


## 6.2 Bone Classification

[OpenStax section 6.2](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/6-2-bone-classification)

- Long bones are longer than wide and often act as levers, such as the femur and phalanges. Classification describes shape rather than absolute size.
- Short bones are roughly cube-shaped, such as most carpals and tarsals. Flat bones provide broad protective or attachment surfaces, as in sternum and cranial vault.
- Irregular bones have complex shapes, such as vertebrae. Sesamoid bones form within tendons and alter force transmission; the patella is the largest example.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Create a five-shape table with definition, two examples where available, and functional relationship. Sort a mixed list of bones without looking at the table.

**Memory cue:** “Long Shorts Fit Irregular Shapes” = Long, Short, Flat, Irregular, Sesamoid. A tiny phalanx is still a long bone.

**Precision check:** A flat bone can be curved. “Long bone” does not mean every physically large bone belongs in that category.


## 6.3 Bone Structure

[OpenStax section 6.3](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/6-3-bone-structure)

- A long bone has diaphysis, epiphyses, metaphyses, articular cartilage, medullary cavity, periosteum, and endosteum. Bone markings include attachment projections, joint surfaces, and passageways.
- Osteogenic cells give rise to osteoblasts. Osteoblasts produce matrix; osteocytes maintain it; osteoclasts resorb it. Organic collagen contributes tensile strength, while inorganic hydroxyapatite contributes hardness.
- Compact bone commonly contains osteons with concentric lamellae around central canals. Osteocytes occupy lacunae and communicate through canaliculi; perforating canals connect vascular routes.
- Spongy bone consists of trabeculae aligned with mechanical demands. Marrow occupies spaces between trabeculae. Nutrient vessels supply living bone cells.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Draw a long bone and enlarged osteon, then compare compact and spongy tissue. Make a bone-marking glossary with one real example per marking.

**Memory cue:** BLASTs Build; CLASTs Clear; CYTEs Care. Lacunae are little rooms; canaliculi connect them.

**Precision check:** Hydroxyapatite is inorganic mineral. Spongy bone is mineralized tissue with spaces, not soft unmineralized bone.


## 6.4 Bone Formation and Development

[OpenStax section 6.4](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/6-4-bone-formation-and-development)

- Intramembranous ossification forms bone within mesenchymal tissue. Endochondral ossification replaces a hyaline cartilage model. Most limb bones use the latter pathway.
- Primary ossification develops in the diaphysis; secondary centers develop in epiphyses. Articular cartilage remains at joint surfaces, and an epiphyseal plate remains during growth.
- OpenStax describes growth-plate zones of reserve cartilage, proliferation, maturation and hypertrophy, and calcified matrix. Cartilage grows toward the epiphyseal side while bone replaces it toward the diaphyseal side.
- Appositional growth increases diameter. Remodeling couples bone resorption and formation throughout life; closure of growth plates ends normal longitudinal growth.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Draw side-by-side ossification timelines and an enlarged growth plate oriented from epiphysis to diaphysis. Show periosteal deposition and endosteal resorption during widening.

**Memory cue:** “Resting People Mature and Calcify” = Reserve, Proliferation, Maturation/hypertrophy, Calcification. “Intra in membrane; Endo replaces cartilage.”

**Precision check:** Cartilage does not simply turn into bone cell-for-cell. The cartilage model is replaced by newly formed osseous tissue.


## 6.5 Fractures: Bone Repair

[OpenStax section 6.5](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/6-5-fractures-bone-repair)

- Classify fractures by pattern and relationship to skin: transverse, oblique, spiral, comminuted, impacted, greenstick, open, and closed. Several descriptors can apply to one fracture.
- Repair commonly proceeds through a hematoma, a fibrocartilaginous or soft callus, a bony or hard callus, and remodeling. Osteoblasts and osteoclasts participate at different stages.
- Reduction restores alignment; immobilization helps maintain conditions for healing. Open reduction and an open fracture describe different things.
- Healing rate and outcome depend on factors including blood supply, stability, extent of injury, nutrition, and age.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Draw four fracture-repair panels with tissue type and participating cells. Sketch each fracture pattern from the textbook and add one distinguishing sentence.

**Memory cue:** “Hematoma Softens; Bone Remodels” = Hematoma, Soft callus, Bony callus, Remodeling.

**Precision check:** A soft callus does not have the mechanical strength of mature remodeled bone. Open reduction refers to a surgical approach, not necessarily a fracture through skin.


## 6.6 Exercise, Nutrition, Hormones, and Bone Tissue

[OpenStax section 6.6](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/6-6-exercise-nutrition-hormones-and-bone-tissue)

- Bone adapts to mechanical loading. Deposition and resorption respond to stress; prolonged unloading can reduce bone mass.
- Calcium and phosphate provide mineral components. Vitamin D supports intestinal calcium absorption; vitamin C supports collagen formation. Protein supplies material for the organic matrix.
- Growth hormone, thyroid hormone, and sex hormones influence bone growth and maturation. Sex hormones contribute to the growth spurt and growth-plate closure.
- PTH, calcitriol, and calcitonin influence calcium regulation. Osteoporosis involves reduced bone mass and structural strength; it differs from defective mineralization.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Make a factor–target–effect table for mechanical load, nutrient roles, growth hormones, and calcium-regulating hormones. Compare osteoporosis with osteomalacia or rickets at the mechanism level.

**Memory cue:** Vitamin C builds Collagen; Vitamin D supports Digestive calcium uptake. “Load lays bone” recalls the response to appropriate mechanical demand.

**Precision check:** The study goal is understanding nutrient and hormone functions, not memorizing supplement doses. Bone quantity and mineralization quality are different variables.


## 6.7 Calcium Homeostasis: Interactions of the Skeletal System and Other Organ Systems

[OpenStax section 6.7](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/6-7-calcium-homeostasis-interactions-of-the-skeletal-system-and-other-organ-systems)

- Extracellular calcium supports neural signaling, muscle contraction, coagulation, and other functions. Bone is a large mineral reservoir, but blood calcium must remain within a narrow regulated range.
- Low blood calcium stimulates PTH release. PTH promotes renal calcium conservation, supports calcitriol formation, and increases bone resorption through signaling to osteoclast-regulating cells.
- Calcitriol increases intestinal calcium absorption. Calcitonin can inhibit osteoclast activity, although it has a smaller day-to-day role in adult calcium regulation than PTH and vitamin D.
- As calcium rises toward its regulated range, the low-calcium stimulus to PTH decreases. The loop links parathyroid glands, bone, kidneys, and intestine.

**Make these notes and drawings:** Draw a low-calcium negative-feedback loop with three target branches: bone, kidney, intestine via calcitriol. Use arrows to show calcium entering or leaving blood.

**Memory cue:** PTH = Puts calcium Higher. CalciTONin can Tone down blood calcium. Always attach the cue to a target organ and mechanism.

**Precision check:** PTH’s stimulation of osteoclast-mediated resorption is indirect. Blood calcium concentration is not a direct measure of total bone strength.

## 7.5 Embryonic Development of the Axial Skeleton

**Precision check:** Do not memorize a single closure age as an exact deadline for every person. Different sutures and fontanelles have different developmental schedules.

## 10.3 Muscle Fiber Contraction and Relaxation

[OpenStax section 10.3](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/10-3-muscle-fiber-contraction-and-relaxation)

ATP binding detaches myosin; ATP hydrolysis repositions the head. Relaxation involves ending ACh signaling and pumping calcium into SR. **Make these notes and drawings:** Draw excitation-contraction coupling, the cross-bridge cycle, and a relaxed/shortened sarcomere. Make an ATP-source comparison.

**Memory cue:** Calcium uncovers; ATP detaches and resets.

**Precision check:** Actin and myosin slide; they do not become shorter filaments.
