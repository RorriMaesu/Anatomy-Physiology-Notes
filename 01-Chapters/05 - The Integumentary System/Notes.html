5.1 Layers of the Skin

OpenStax section 5.1

  • Skin consists of epidermis and dermis. The hypodermis lies beneath skin and contains areolar and adipose tissue. The epidermis is avascular keratinized stratified squamous epithelium.
  • From deep to superficial, epidermal strata are basale, spinosum, granulosum, lucidum when present, and corneum. Thick skin on palms and soles has lucidum and lacks hair follicles.
  • Keratinocytes form the protective barrier; melanocytes produce melanin; epidermal dendritic cells support defense; tactile epithelial cells contribute to touch sensation.
  • Papillary dermis contains areolar connective tissue; reticular dermis contains dense irregular connective tissue. Blood supply, nerves, and many accessory structures lie in the dermis. Pigmentation reflects melanin, carotene, and blood-related influences.

Make these notes and drawings: Draw a skin section with every stratum, both dermal layers, hypodermis, vessels, and major cell types. Compare thick and thin skin. Connect surface cell loss with basal replacement.

Memory cue: “Come Let’s Get Sun Burned” = Corneum, Lucidum, Granulosum, Spinosum, Basale, SUPERFICIAL to DEEP. Omit Lucidum for thin skin.

Precision check: The reticular dermis is dense irregular connective tissue. The hypodermis is not an epidermal layer.

5.2 Accessory Structures of the Skin

OpenStax section 5.2

  • Hair develops from dividing matrix cells in a follicle; the visible shaft is dead keratinized material. Label root, bulb, papilla, matrix, and arrector pili. Hair cycles through growth, regression, and rest.
  • Nails grow from a matrix and contain hard keratin. Distinguish nail plate, bed, root, lunula, and cuticle.
  • Eccrine sweat glands open onto the skin surface and support cooling. Apocrine sweat glands usually empty into hair follicles in selected areas and become active around puberty.
  • Sebaceous glands release lipid-rich sebum, usually into follicles, by holocrine secretion. Their lubricating secretion differs from watery sweat.

Make these notes and drawings: Make a hair-and-nail labeling page plus a gland comparison table with secretion, outlet, location, and function. Draw an eccrine duct to the surface and sebaceous duct to a follicle.

Memory cue: Eccrine Exits onto Epidermis; Sebaceous supplies Sebum. Hair “Grows, Changes, Rests” recalls anagen, catagen, telogen.

Precision check: Do not infer a gland’s secretion mechanism solely from its name. Human apocrine sweat glands principally use exocytotic secretion despite the historical name.

5.3 Functions of the Integumentary System

OpenStax section 5.3

  • The skin limits water loss and protects against mechanical injury, chemicals, microbes, and some ultraviolet damage. Barrier function depends on cells, keratin, lipids, and immune defenses.
  • Receptors detect touch, pressure, vibration, temperature, and potentially damaging stimuli. Different receptors occupy different depths and have different response properties.
  • Sweating cools through evaporation. Dermal vasodilation increases heat transfer toward the surface; vasoconstriction reduces it. Hypodermal fat contributes insulation.
  • Ultraviolet exposure initiates production of a vitamin D precursor in skin; liver and kidney processing contributes to formation of active vitamin D. Skin aging affects barrier, elasticity, sensation, and repair.

Make these notes and drawings: Draw warm- and cold-response feedback loops. Make a structure–function table linking keratin, melanin, blood vessels, glands, receptors, and adipose tissue to their jobs.

Memory cue: PSTV = Protect, Sense, Thermoregulate, Vitamin D. “Dilate to Dissipate” recalls heat loss through increased skin blood flow.

Precision check: Sweat must evaporate to provide effective cooling. Sweat production alone is not the same as heat removal.

5.4 Diseases, Disorders, and Injuries of the Integumentary System

OpenStax section 5.4

  • Compare basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and melanoma by cells of origin and patterns of spread. Melanoma arises from melanocytes and can metastasize.
  • Superficial burns involve epidermis; partial-thickness burns extend into dermis; full-thickness burns destroy epidermis and dermis. Extent and location also affect severity.
  • Distinguish eczema, acne, pressure injuries, stretch marks, calluses, scars, and keloids by the structural change and mechanism described in the text.
  • Loss of the skin barrier can increase fluid loss and infection risk. Deep damage may heal with fibrosis and loss of accessory structures.

Make these notes and drawings: Create disorder–affected structure–mechanism–functional consequence notes. Draw three burn depths. Learn the adult rule-of-nines diagram if assigned, including its limits for children.

Memory cue: ABCDE recalls melanoma recognition features: Asymmetry, Border irregularity, Color variation, Diameter, Evolving. It is a screening memory cue, not a diagnosis or a rule excluding small melanomas.

Precision check: A deeper burn may damage sensory endings and reduce sensation locally. Pain intensity alone does not reliably identify depth.
